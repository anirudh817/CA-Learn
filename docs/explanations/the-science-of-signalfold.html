How to read this

SignalFold takes a spreadsheet of protein measurements and turns it into a biological story: what changed between two groups of samples, which proteins behave as a team, what those teams do for a living, and which cell types they point to. This document explains the science that makes that possible — slowly, from the ground up.

The whole pipeline in one sentence

Measure thousands of proteins in two groups of samples → make the samples fairly comparable → find the proteins that differ and the proteins that rise and fall together → ask what those groups of proteins do and which cell types they belong to.

1 · The big picture

Modern instruments can measure the amount of thousands of different proteins at once in a single biological sample — a tube of blood, a piece of tissue, a sample of spinal fluid. Run that instrument on, say, 10 healthy people and 10 patients, and you get a giant table: thousands of rows (proteins), twenty columns (samples), each cell a number for “how much of this protein was here.”

That table is exciting and useless at the same time. Exciting, because the answer to a medical question is probably hiding in it. Useless, because no human can stare at 20,000 numbers × 20 columns and see the answer. SignalFold is the machine that turns the table into understanding. It does so by answering four questions in order:

Q1 — What changed? Which individual proteins are present at clearly different levels in patients versus healthy controls? differential expression
Q2 — What moves together? Which proteins rise and fall in lockstep across samples, suggesting they work as a team? co-expression network
Q3 — What does it mean? What biological jobs (functions, pathways) are those teams responsible for? GO enrichment
Q4 — Which cells? Which cell types do these proteins belong to, telling us which populations of cells are involved? cell-type enrichment

The four questions map onto a fixed sequence of steps. Data is cleaned first, then Stage 1 answers Q1 and Q2, Stage 2 answers Q3, and Stage 3 answers Q4:

Mental model

Think of SignalFold as a detective working a case. Stage 1 gathers the evidence (which proteins look suspicious, and which ones keep showing up together). Stage 2 asks what those suspects do — their occupations and known associates. Stage 3 asks where they live — which cell-type neighbourhood they come from. The final report ties motive, method, and location into one narrative.

The rest of this tutorial walks each box of that diagram, in order. We begin with the two things you must understand before any of it makes sense: a little biology, and what the data actually is.

2 · Biology from zero

You can follow this whole document with just a handful of biology ideas. Here they are.

Cells, genes, and proteins

Your body is built from tiny units called cells. Almost every cell carries the same master instruction set, written in a molecule called DNA. You can picture DNA as an enormous cookbook that every cell owns a copy of.

A gene is one recipe in that cookbook — the instructions for making one particular thing. The “thing” a recipe produces is usually a protein. Proteins are the molecules that actually do the work of life: they digest food, carry oxygen, fight infections, give cells their shape, send signals. If DNA is the cookbook and a gene is a recipe, a protein is the finished dish that does a job at the dinner table.

Getting from recipe to dish happens in two steps, together called the central dogma of biology:

“Expression” is just an amount

When biologists say a gene or protein is highly expressed, they simply mean “there is a lot of it.” Expression level = abundance = how many copies are present. This is the single most important word in this document, because every stage of SignalFold is ultimately reasoning about amounts and how they differ.

Crucially, even though every cell has the same DNA, cells make wildly different amounts of each protein depending on what they are and what is happening to them. A liver cell and a brain cell run different recipes at different volumes. A sick cell makes different amounts than a healthy one. Those differences are the signal we are hunting.

Why proteins, and not genes?

You may have heard of genomics (reading DNA) or transcriptomics (measuring mRNA). SignalFold is a proteomics platform: it measures the proteins themselves. Proteins are one step closer to what a cell actually does than DNA or RNA are — the recipe and the working copy matter, but it is the finished dish that feeds you. Measuring proteins is harder than measuring RNA, but it is often more directly relevant to disease and to drugs (most drugs target proteins).

Plain English

Gene symbol. Each human gene/protein has a short official nickname, written in capitals: APOE, GFAP, TP53. Throughout SignalFold, proteins are usually referred to by these symbols. You do not need to know what they stand for — treat them as names, like surnames in a phone book.

In SignalFold

The whole pipeline is organism-aware. By default it expects human proteins identified by gene symbol, and it also ships a mouse reference for pre-clinical studies. The biology references it leans on later (functional categories in Stage 2, cell-type markers in Stage 3) are likewise human-by-default.

3 · The raw material: your data

How protein amounts get measured

You do not need the engineering details, but a one-paragraph picture helps. There are two broad ways to measure thousands of proteins:

  • Mass spectrometry chops proteins into small fragments (called peptides) and effectively weighs them with extreme precision. The heavier the signal for a given protein, the more of it was present. Instruments and software in this family include PEAKS, Spectronaut, MaxQuant, and DIA-NN.
  • Affinity panels use designer molecules that stick only to specific target proteins and light up in proportion to how much is there. Olink is the best-known example.

Either way, the end product is the same shape: a big table of numbers. SignalFold does not care which instrument you used — it only needs that table.

The table itself

The central object is a grid called the abundance matrix: one row per protein, one column per sample, and in each cell, how much of that protein was measured in that sample. The samples are split into the two groups you want to compare.

The experiment behind the table

Three design ideas are baked into how SignalFold reads your data:

  • Two groups (cohorts). The pipeline compares one group against another — classically a control group against a disease (or treated) group. By default these are literally named Control and Disease, and SignalFold can guess which sample belongs to which group from the column names.
  • Replicates. You never measure just one control and one patient. You measure several of each — these repeats are replicates. They are what let statistics tell a real difference from random noise. The engine requires at least 2 samples per group to run, and by default a protein must be measured in at least 3 samples to be kept at all.
  • Traits (optional). You can attach a second small table of clinical measurements per sample — age, a lab value, a disease-severity score. In SignalFold these are called traits, and Stage 1 can later test whether any protein team tracks them. (For example, Alzheimer's studies often attach tau and amyloid-β measurements.)
Plain English

Protein vs. peptide. Mass spectrometers actually see peptides (the chopped-up fragments), not whole proteins. Some files report results already rolled up to whole proteins (“protein level”); others report the individual fragments (“peptide level”). SignalFold handles both — it just calls each row a “feature” so the same machinery works either way.

In SignalFold — the formats it understands

You can upload a CSV, TSV, or Excel file. SignalFold auto-detects which instrument it came from and adjusts sensible defaults accordingly:

Plain English — what is NPX?

NPX (Normalized Protein eXpression) is Olink's unit. Two facts are enough: (1) it is a relative measure of abundance, not an absolute concentration, so it is meant for comparing samples within one study; and (2) it is already on a log₂ scale — each +1 NPX means roughly a doubling of the protein. SignalFold knows NPX is pre-logged and will not log it a second time.

4 · Cleaning the data (ETL)

Before any analysis, SignalFold runs a preparation step often called ETL (Extract, Transform, Load). It is unglamorous and absolutely essential: every later result is only as trustworthy as the table that feeds it. Three jobs happen here.

4.1 Fixing the names

Protein names arrive messy. The most infamous problem: open a gene list in Excel and it “helpfully” converts gene symbols into dates. The gene SEPT2 becomes 2-Sep; MARCH1 becomes 1-Mar. This corrupted so many published datasets that the official naming body eventually renamed some genes. SignalFold detects these mangled names and converts them back. It also maps cryptic database IDs to the standard human gene symbol so the same protein is never split across two different names.

Watch out

If the same protein appears under two names, every downstream count is wrong: it looks like two proteins, enrichment tallies are off, and a real hit can be diluted into invisibility. Name-cleaning is not pedantry — it protects the statistics.

4.2 Dropping the unreliable rows

Real instruments miss things. A protein might be measured in some samples and come back blank in others. A row that is mostly blanks cannot support a trustworthy comparison, so SignalFold filters it out using two simple rules.

In SignalFold

Missing-value threshold (default 0.5): a protein may be blank in at most 50% of samples. Minimum samples present (default 3): a protein must have a real measurement in at least 3 samples. Rows failing either rule are removed before anything else happens. Both are adjustable.

4.3 Putting numbers on a sensible scale: the log₂ transform

Raw protein amounts span an enormous range — some proteins are thousands of times more abundant than others. Worse, the numbers are skewed: a handful of giant values and a huge crowd of small ones. Two problems follow. First, the giants dominate every calculation. Second, “change” is multiplicative in biology (a protein doubles or halves), but raw subtraction treats a jump from 10→20 as trivial next to 1000→1010, even though the first is a doubling and the second is nothing.

The fix is the logarithm, base 2. Taking log₂ of every value compresses the giants, spreads out the crowd, and — the magic part — turns multiplication into addition. On a log₂ scale, a doubling is always +1, whether you go 10→20 or 1000→2000. That makes “fold change” (the next section) clean and symmetric.

In SignalFold

The app inspects your numbers and auto-detects whether they already look log-scaled. If they are raw (large, skewed), it applies log₂(value + 1) — the “+1” is a small trick so that zeros don't blow up the logarithm. If they already look logged (like Olink NPX), it leaves them alone. You can override this with the log handling switch.

5 · Normalization — making samples comparable

This is the first step of Stage 1, and the first place statistics enters.

The problem. Even two identical samples never give identical totals. One tube had slightly more material loaded; the instrument drifted between Monday and Friday; one batch of reagent was a touch stronger. These technical differences have nothing to do with biology — but they sit right on top of the biological signal we care about.

Here is the analogy. Imagine ten photographers shoot the same landscape, each with their camera set to a different brightness. If you compare raw photos, you will “discover” that some skies are darker — but that is the camera, not the weather. Before you can compare the scenes, you must put every photo on the same brightness. Normalization is that brightness adjustment for samples.

The goal is delicate: remove the technical offsets without erasing the biological ones. SignalFold does this by gently sliding each sample's numbers up or down so the samples line up, leaving the internal pattern of each sample intact.

The menu of methods

SignalFold offers several recipes for “lining up,” differing in how aggressive they are:

In SignalFold

The Normalization Method setting defaults to median — the robust, hard-to-go-wrong choice. For Olink data (already NPX-normalized and log-scaled), if you ask for TIN the app sensibly falls back to median, because dividing by a total makes no sense on a log scale.

Watch out — the key assumption

Lining up whole-sample distributions assumes that most proteins don't actually change between samples, so any global shift must be technical. That is usually true. But if your two groups are radically different at a global level (e.g. one has far more total protein for a real biological reason), aggressive normalization like quantile can scrub away genuine signal. When in doubt, the gentler median is safer.

6 · Differential expression — what changed?

This answers Q1. For every single protein, we ask: is its level meaningfully different between the two groups? Two numbers decide it — how big the difference is, and how sure we are it isn't luck.

6.1 How big: fold change

Fold change is just a ratio: “how many times more (or less) in disease than in control.” If a protein averages 100 in patients and 50 in controls, that is a 2-fold increase. Because we work on a log₂ scale, SignalFold reports the log₂ fold change (often “log2FC”), computed simply as (average in disease) − (average in control).

log2FC = mean(Disease) − mean(Control) Reading it: +1 = doubled, +2 = quadrupled, +3 = 8×.   −1 = halved.   0 = no change. The sign tells you direction; the size tells you magnitude.

From our toy matrix, APOE went from ~10 to ~13 on the log₂ scale — a log2FC of about +3, i.e. roughly an 8-fold increase in disease. That is enormous. ACTB stayed flat (log2FC ≈ 0).

6.2 How sure: the p-value

A big difference in the averages might still be a fluke of which patients you happened to sample. Statistics quantifies that doubt with a p-value.

Plain English — what a p-value is

Pretend, just for argument, that a protein truly does not differ between the groups. The p-value is the probability that pure chance would hand you a difference at least as large as the one you saw. Small p (say below 0.05) = “chance alone would rarely do this”, so the difference is probably real. Large p = “eh, could easily be noise.”

To compute that probability, SignalFold runs a statistical test on each protein, comparing the two groups' values. You can choose between two:

  • t-test default — compares the two group averages while accounting for how spread out the values are and how many samples you have. (Specifically a Welch t-test, which doesn't assume the two groups are equally variable.) Works best when values are roughly bell-shaped.
  • Wilcoxon / Mann–Whitney — a rank-based test. Instead of using the raw numbers it just asks “do disease values tend to rank higher than control values?” It makes no bell-curve assumption and shrugs off outliers, at the cost of some power when samples are few.

6.3 The trap of testing thousands of proteins at once

Here is a subtlety that catches everyone. A p-value below 0.05 means “chance would do this less than 5% of the time.” Fine for one test. But SignalFold runs the test on thousands of proteins. If you test 20,000 proteins that are all pure noise, 5% of them — about 1,000 proteins — will sail under p < 0.05 by chance alone. Your “hits” would be mostly garbage.

Plain English — false discovery rate (FDR)

The fix is to adjust the p-values for the sheer number of tests. SignalFold uses the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, which controls the False Discovery Rate: the expected fraction of your declared hits that are actually false. Setting FDR < 0.05 means: “of the proteins I call significant, on average no more than 5% should be mistakes.” The adjusted value is often called a q-value or adjusted p-value.

This is gentler and smarter than the old, harsh Bonferroni approach (which demands near-impossible certainty and throws away real findings). FDR keeps you honest without making you blind. SignalFold offers Bonferroni too, for the rare case you need that severity.

6.4 The verdict, and the volcano plot

A protein is flagged significant only if it clears both bars at once:

significant = ( |log2FC| ≥ log₂(1.5) ) AND ( adjusted p < 0.05 ) Big enough to matter and sure enough to believe. Either one alone is not enough. log₂(1.5) ≈ 0.585.

The classic way to see all proteins at once is the volcano plot. Each dot is one protein. Left–right shows fold change (direction and size); up–down shows significance (how small the p-value is). The proteins that matter erupt up and out to the sides — hence “volcano.”

In SignalFold — the knobs

Statistical test = t-test (default) or Wilcoxon. p-value threshold = 0.05. Fold-change threshold = 1.5×. Use BH-adjusted p-value (FDR) = on by default, with the correction method set to Benjamini–Hochberg (Bonferroni available). The output is the volcano table — every protein with its log2FC, raw p, adjusted p, and an up / down / not-significant call — plus ready-made lists of the up- and down-regulated hits.

Watch out

Significant ≠ important. With enough samples, a tiny, biologically meaningless change can earn a microscopic p-value. That is exactly why SignalFold demands a real fold change too. Conversely, a huge fold change with a weak p-value (few, noisy samples) is not yet trustworthy. Always read both axes of the volcano together — never one alone.

7 · Co-expression networks (WGCNA)

This answers Q2, and it is the most powerful idea in the pipeline. The method is called WGCNA — Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis.

The idea: guilt by association. Differential expression looks at proteins one at a time. But proteins don't work alone — they work in teams (pathways, machines, complexes). If two proteins rise and fall together across your samples, that synchrony is a strong hint they belong to the same team. Find the teams, and you've found the biology.

7.1 “Move together” means correlated

The raw signal is correlation: do two proteins' levels track each other across samples? When sample 3 is high for protein A and also high for protein B, and sample 5 is low for both, they are correlated. When there's no such pattern, they aren't.

7.2 From pairs to a network, carefully

WGCNA computes a togetherness score for every pair of proteins and treats the result as a network: proteins are dots, strong correlations are connections. But it does three clever things rather than naively drawing a line wherever correlation is high:

  1. Robust correlation (bicor). Instead of the ordinary correlation (which a single weird sample can distort), SignalFold defaults to the biweight midcorrelation — same idea, but it quietly down-weights outliers so one odd sample can't manufacture a fake relationship.
  2. Soft thresholding (the power β). Rather than a hard “connected / not connected” cutoff, every correlation is raised to a power (default β = 8). Raising numbers between 0 and 1 to the 8th power barely touches strong correlations but crushes weak ones toward zero. The effect: faint, probably-coincidental links fade out, strong links remain. This tuning is what makes the network “scale-free” — a realistic shape where a few proteins are highly connected hubs and most have only a few links (more on this in the Deep Dive).
  3. Topological overlap (TOM): counting mutual friends. Two proteins are judged truly close not only if they are directly correlated, but if they share the same neighbours. Just like two people who have dozens of friends in common are probably in the same circle even if they've never met, proteins with many shared partners get grouped together. This “mutual friends” measure makes the grouping far more robust than raw correlation alone.

You can also choose whether direction matters. A signed network (the default) treats “both go up together” as related but “one up, one down” as not; an unsigned network treats any strong relationship as a link regardless of direction.

7.3 The payoff: modules

Once every protein pair has a robust togetherness score, WGCNA clusters the proteins — repeatedly merging the closest ones — until distinct groups fall out. Each group is a module: a set of proteins that move as one. By long-standing convention, modules are named by colour (turquoise, blue, brown, yellow…). Proteins that don't fit any module are dumped into a leftover bin called grey.

7.4 Summarising and labelling a module

Two more ideas make modules usable:

  • Module eigengene. A module might have 300 proteins. Their shared up-and-down pattern can be distilled into a single representative profile — the module eigengene. Think of it as the module's “theme song”: one line that captures how the whole module rises and falls across samples. It lets us treat a 300-protein module as one tidy variable.
  • Membership (kME) and hubs. Not every member belongs equally. kME measures how tightly a protein follows its module's theme song (from 0 to 1). High-kME proteins are the hubs — the core, most-connected members, and the best candidates for “the protein that matters most” in that module.

7.5 Linking modules to the disease: module–trait correlation

Now the magic. Because each module has a single eigengene (theme song), we can correlate that one number against the things we care about — disease status, and any clinical traits you uploaded. A module whose theme song rises steadily with disease severity is a module worth investigating.

In SignalFold — the WGCNA knobs

Soft power β = 8. Correlation = bicor (robust). Network type = signed. Minimum module size = 20 proteins. Deep split = 3 and merge height = 0.30 control how finely the clusters are cut and merged. You can also let the app auto-pick β by a “scale-free fit” criterion instead of fixing it. Outputs include each protein's module and kME, the hub lists, the eigengenes, and the module–trait table behind the heatmap.

Watch out

Modules are discovered from your data, not handed down as truth — a different cohort may yield somewhat different modules. Co-expression also shows association, not causation: a hub is a great lead, not a proven driver. And WGCNA needs a decent number of samples to find stable patterns; with very few samples, treat modules as hypotheses. (For speed and memory, the network is built on the most variable proteins — up to 5,000 — while differential expression in section 6 still runs on every protein.)

8 · Functional enrichment (GO) — what does it mean?

This answers Q3. Stage 1 handed us modules — teams of co-moving proteins — but a team is just a list of names until we know what it does. Stage 2 attaches biological meaning.

8.1 A dictionary of biological jobs: the Gene Ontology

Biologists have spent decades building the Gene Ontology (GO): a huge, carefully curated catalogue that tags every known protein with the jobs it performs. GO has three branches:

  • Biological Process (BP) — the goal a protein contributes to. E.g. “immune response,” “cell death,” “synaptic signalling.”
  • Molecular Function (MF) — the molecular action it performs. E.g. “ATP binding,” “enzyme activity.”
  • Cellular Component (CC) — where in the cell it acts. E.g. “mitochondrion,” “cell membrane.”

SignalFold also folds in curated pathway collections (Reactome, KEGG, Hallmark) alongside GO. Together these are the “job descriptions” we'll test each module against.

8.2 The question: more than you'd expect by chance?

The core technique is over-representation analysis. For one module and one job description (say “immune response”), we ask: does this module contain noticeably more immune-response proteins than a random handful of proteins would? If yes, the module is “enriched” for immune response — that's its biology.

Plain English — the bag-of-marbles picture

Put every measured protein into a bag. Some marbles are painted — they're the “immune response” proteins. Your module is a handful you scooped out. If the bag is 2% painted but your handful is 8% painted, you probably didn't scoop at random — something drew immune proteins together. The statistical test that puts a number on “probably” is Fisher's exact test.

8.3 Three details that keep it honest

  • Which bag? (the background universe). Enrichment is always relative to a background. SignalFold's default is the proteins you actually measured, not every protein in existence — because only measured proteins ever had a chance to land in a module. Using the right background prevents inflated, misleading results.
  • Correct for many terms (FDR again). We test thousands of job descriptions against every module, so the same multiple-testing trap from section 6 returns. SignalFold applies Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across all the tests and reports only terms below the cutoff.
  • Trim the duplicates. GO terms overlap heavily (“immune response” vs “adaptive immune response” vs “defense response”). Reporting all of them is noise. SignalFold uses a similarity measure (Cohen's kappa) to collapse near-duplicate terms down to one representative, so you read a clean list of distinct themes.

Beyond a yes/no call, SignalFold also reports a signed enrichment score (a z-score) for each term — positive when the module is enriched, negative when it is unusually depleted of that job — with larger magnitude meaning stronger evidence. This is handy for ranking and for heatmaps across modules.

In SignalFold

GO categories = BP + MF + CC (toggle each). FDR threshold = 0.05. Minimum hits per term = 3 (don't test a job description that barely any measured protein could match). Background = measured features (or the full database, your choice). Redundancy removal = kappa. The gene-set definitions come from a bundled, dated human collection of GO + pathways, and you can supply your own.

Watch out

Over-representation is membership-based: it counts which proteins are in the module, but ignores how much each one changed. (A different family of methods, GSEA, uses the magnitudes — see the Deep Dive.) Also, very broad terms like “metabolic process” can light up easily and tell you little; the specific, mid-sized terms are usually the informative ones. And remember: change the background and the p-values change — enrichment is always a statement relative to the bag you chose.

9 · Cell-type enrichment — which cells?

This answers Q4, the final layer of interpretation.

The problem of the blend. A tissue sample is not one kind of cell — it's a mixture. Brain tissue, for instance, is a blend of neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and blood-vessel (endothelial) cells. When you grind up tissue and measure proteins, you get the combined signal of all of them. So when a protein team changes, a natural question is: did one cell population change — grow, shrink, or switch on — and which one?

9.1 Marker proteins are cell-type name tags

Each cell type reliably makes certain signature proteins that other cells barely make — its markers. GFAP marks astrocytes; proteins like IBA1 mark microglia; MBP marks oligodendrocytes. Researchers have compiled curated lists of these markers per cell type. They act like name tags: if a protein team is stuffed with astrocyte markers, the team is probably an astrocyte story.

9.2 The same test, a new question

Here's the elegant part: Stage 3 reuses the exact same Fisher's exact test from Stage 2. The only thing that changes is what we test against. Instead of asking “does this module overlap the ‘immune response’ gene set more than chance?” we ask “does this module overlap the astrocyte marker set more than chance?” Run that for every module against every cell type, correct for multiple testing with FDR, and you get a map of module → cell type.

In SignalFold

The default human reference covers five brain cell types — astrocytes, microglia, neurons, oligodendrocytes, and endothelia — with marker lists drawn from a curated union of published single-cell references (the Sharma/Zhang union, built on resources like PanglaoDB and CellMarker). A mouse reference is included, and you can upload your own marker file for other tissues. Significance again uses Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR; results are shown as a module×cell-type heatmap of −log₁₀(FDR).

Watch out

This is enrichment, not true “deconvolution” — it tells you which cell-type signatures are over-represented in a module, not the exact percentage of each cell type in your sample. Markers can be shared between related cell types, so a module can light up for more than one. And the built-in references are brain-focused; for liver, blood, or tumour work you'll want to supply matching markers, or the map will be uninformative.

10 · A worked example, end to end

Let's watch the same cast of proteins travel through every stage, in a realistic (if simplified) Alzheimer's-style study. This is where the four questions become one narrative.

The setup. A lab measures the proteins in spinal fluid from 10 healthy people and 10 Alzheimer's patients, and also records two pathology markers per person — tau and amyloid-β — as traits. They upload the abundance table and the traits, leave the defaults, and run SignalFold.

Notice that the conclusion is something no single number could have given you. A volcano plot alone would have left you with a list of proteins and no theme. The modules, the functional labels, and the cell-type map turn a list into an explanation: in these patients, a population of astrocytes appears activated and is mounting a complement-driven inflammatory response that scales with the hallmark pathology. That is a hypothesis a scientist can act on.

Where this is useful

  • Biomarker discovery — find proteins (and protein teams) that separate patients from controls and track disease severity, e.g. in neurodegeneration with tau/amyloid traits.
  • Mechanism & cell-type implication — move beyond “what changed” to “what process, in which cells” — the difference between a list and an explanation.

  • Hypothesis generation — the modules and enrichments are leads to validate with targeted follow-up experiments.

11 · Deep dive

Everything above was the “what” and “why.” This section adds the “exactly how,” the assumptions that can bite you, and the formulas — for readers who want to reason about the methods, not just use them. It is denser by design. Section 12 lists where to read further.

11.1 Hypothesis testing, properly

Every significance test shares one logic. You state a null hypothesis H₀ (“this protein does not differ between groups”) and an alternative H₁ (“it does”). You compute a test statistic that summarises the evidence, then ask how surprising that statistic would be if H₀ were true. That surprise, expressed as a probability, is the p-value.

For two groups, the workhorse is the two-sample t-test. SignalFold uses the Welch variant, which — unlike Student's original — does not assume the two groups have equal variance:

t = ( x̄₁ − x̄₂ ) / √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ ) x̄ = group mean, s² = group variance, n = group size. Bigger mean-gap → bigger |t|. More scatter or fewer samples → smaller |t|. The t value is converted to a p-value via the t-distribution.

The Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) alternative discards the raw values and works only with their ranks, testing whether one group's values tend to outrank the other's. It assumes far less (no bell curve) and resists outliers, but with very small samples it can lack the resolution to reach significance at all.

Two errors, and power

A Type I error (false positive) is calling a difference real when it isn't — the rate is the threshold you set (e.g. 0.05). A Type II error (false negative) is missing a real difference. Statistical power is 1 − the Type II rate: your chance of catching a true effect. Power rises with more replicates, larger effects, and less noise — which is why replicate count is the single biggest lever on what a study can find.

11.2 Multiple testing and the false discovery rate

Run one test at α = 0.05 and your false-positive risk is 5%. Run m independent tests and the chance of at least one false positive is 1 − (0.95)m — which is essentially 100% by the time m is in the thousands. There are two philosophies for controlling this:

  • Family-wise error rate (FWER) — control the probability of any false positive. Bonferroni does this by testing each hypothesis at α/m. Safe, but brutally conservative: with m = 20,000 you'd need p < 0.0000025, burying most real findings.
  • False discovery rate (FDR) — control the expected proportion of your declared discoveries that are false. Far more powerful for screening thousands of features, at the cost of tolerating a known, small fraction of mistakes.

SignalFold defaults to the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) FDR procedure. The rule is simple to state: sort the m p-values ascending, p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(m); find the largest rank k for which

p(k) ≤ (k / m) × α Then reject (call significant) all hypotheses with rank 1…k. The “adjusted p-value” or “q-value” reported per feature is the smallest FDR at which that feature would be called — convenient because you can threshold it directly at 0.05.

BH assumes the tests are independent or positively correlated — generally fine for omics. Note that SignalFold corrects globally within each stage (all module×term tests pooled together in Stage 2, all module×cell-type tests in Stage 3), which is the conservative, defensible choice.

11.3 Normalization, and what each method quietly assumes

Every normalization encodes a belief about what is “technical” vs “biological.” Choosing one is choosing an assumption:

  • Median / mean assume a single per-sample shift (location offset) explains the technical difference — equivalently, that most proteins don't truly change, so the bulk centre should align. Median is the robust form (the breakdown point of the median is 50%; one wild value can't move it).
  • Quantile assumes every sample should share an identical distribution, not just an identical centre. It is the most aggressive option: extremely effective at removing technical structure, but it will erase a genuine global difference between groups if one exists. Use it when you trust that samples are exchangeable in distribution.
  • Z-score (per sample) removes both location and scale, re-expressing values in standard-deviation units. This discards absolute-level information and is best when you care about co-variation patterns (as the network does) rather than magnitudes.
  • TIN (total-intensity) assumes the summed signal per sample should be constant, directly modelling “amount loaded.” Natural for label-free mass spec; meaningless on an already-log, already-normalized scale like Olink NPX (which is why SignalFold redirects it to median there).

Methods you'll meet elsewhere and may want for harder cases: VSN (variance-stabilizing normalization, which also tames the mean–variance relationship) and cyclic loess (which corrects intensity-dependent biases). They are not built in here but are standard further reading.

11.4 Ordinary t-tests vs. moderated statistics (limma)

It is worth being precise about what SignalFold's Stage 1 actually computes: an ordinary, per-protein Welch t-test (or Wilcoxon), each protein tested in isolation, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction afterwards. This is transparent and makes minimal assumptions, but in the small-sample regime typical of proteomics (say 3–6 per group) a single protein's variance estimate is itself noisy — and a protein that happens to look near-constant by chance can earn a spuriously tiny p-value.

The most widely used remedy is limma's empirical-Bayes moderation. It fits the same linear model per protein but then borrows strength across all proteins: each protein's variance is shrunk toward a global trend, producing a moderated t-statistic whose denominator is a compromise between the protein's own scatter and the proteome-wide average. The result is more stable and more powerful when replicates are few, and it is the de-facto standard for differential expression in both transcriptomics and proteomics. If you outgrow plain t-tests, this is the natural next step; the tradeoff is an added modelling assumption (a shared prior on variances).

11.5 The mathematics of WGCNA

Adjacency. From the (robust, bicor) correlation cij between proteins i and j, a weighted connection is

unsigned: aij = | cij |β signed: aij = ( (1 + cij) / 2 )β β is the soft-thresholding power (default 8). In a signed network, anti-correlated proteins (c ≈ −1) map to a ≈ 0 — opposite behaviour is treated as “not connected.”

Why a power, and why 8? Raising correlations to a power suppresses weak links far more than strong ones, pushing the network toward scale-free topology — the empirically observed shape of biological networks, where most proteins have few connections and a handful of hubs have many. The pickSoftThreshold routine scans candidate β values and selects the smallest one whose network's degree distribution fits a scale-free model with R² above a cutoff (SignalFold's auto mode uses 0.8; the default fixed value is 8). The curve below shows the mechanism.

Topological overlap. Direct adjacency is then refined into the Topological Overlap Measure, which rewards shared neighbours:

TOMij = ( Σu aiuauj + aij ) / ( min(ki, kj) + 1 − aij ) ki = total connectivity of protein i (its summed adjacencies). The numerator counts the partners i and j share. Clustering then runs on the dissimilarity 1 − TOM.

From dissimilarity to modules. Average-linkage hierarchical clustering on (1 − TOM) yields a dendrogram; the Dynamic Tree Cut algorithm carves it into modules, governed by deepSplit (sensitivity, 0–4; default 3) and minModuleSize (default 20). Modules whose eigengenes are very similar are then merged if their correlation exceeds 1 − mergeCutHeight (default merge height 0.30), and an optional PAM step tidies borderline assignments.

Eigengene, membership, trait link. A module's eigengene (ME) is the first principal component of its standardized member profiles — the single direction capturing the most shared variance. Module membership is kMEi = cor(xi, ME); high-|kME| proteins are hubs. Module–trait association is just cor(ME, trait), with a p-value from the standard correlation t-test (and the same caution about sample size applies).

Parameter sensitivity

Module results move with β, deepSplit, mergeCutHeight, and minModuleSize. There is no single “correct” setting — bigger β and bigger min-size give fewer, cleaner modules; higher deepSplit gives more, smaller ones. Treat the defaults as a sensible starting point and confirm that your key module is robust to reasonable changes. Also recall the network is built on up to the 5,000 most-variable proteins for tractability, so very low-variance proteins are absent from modules (they remain “grey”) even though they were tested for differential expression.

11.6 Over-representation: the exact test and its cousins

The enrichment p-value comes from the hypergeometric distribution — the “draw without replacement” model — which is exactly the one-sided form of Fisher's exact test. With universe size N, term size K, module size n, and observed overlap a:

P(X = a) = [ C(K, a) · C(N−K, n−a) ] / C(N, n) C(·,·) is “n-choose-k.” The enrichment p-value is the upper tail P(X ≥ a) = Σx ≥ a P(X = x): the chance of an overlap at least this large if the module were a random draw. SignalFold computes the one-sided “greater” test.

Being exact (rather than the χ² approximation) matters here because overlaps are often tiny — a handful of genes — where approximations are unreliable.

  • ORA vs GSEA. Over-representation analysis needs a cut list (here, the module membership) and counts overlaps — it ignores how strongly each protein changed. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) instead walks the entire ranked list and detects gene sets that drift coherently toward one end, catching coordinated subtle shifts ORA misses. SignalFold's Stage 2 is ORA, applied per module — a natural fit, since modules are themselves the “interesting” gene lists.
  • The universe is a real choice. Using all measured proteins (default) vs the full annotation database changes N, and therefore every p-value. The measured-feature background is the honest one for “what's special about this module among proteins we could have seen.”
  • Signed z-score (piano-style). To rank and to draw cross-module heatmaps, SignalFold converts each result to a signed z by running both the “greater” (enrichment) and “less” (depletion) tests and mapping the smaller p through the inverse normal, with the sign showing direction. One number then carries both “which way” and “how strongly.”
  • Redundancy via Cohen's kappa. Term gene-sets overlap heavily; κ measures agreement between two sets beyond chance, and terms above a similarity threshold are collapsed to a single representative so the report shows distinct themes rather than twenty rewordings of one.

11.7 Cell-type enrichment: specifics and limits

Mechanically identical to Stage 2 — one-sided Fisher's exact test, global BH–FDR — but the gene sets are cell-type marker lists and the visual metric is −log₁₀(FDR) (clamped to avoid numerical underflow, since the best overlaps can produce astronomically small p-values).

  • Enrichment, not proportions. This identifies which cell-type signatures are over-represented in a module. It does not estimate the fraction of each cell type in your sample. For true proportion estimates you need dedicated deconvolution methods (e.g. reference-based regression like CIBERSORT), which are a different tool for a different question — good further reading if percentages are what you need.
  • The universe definition bites here. How duplicate gene names are counted (“allow” vs “collapse”) changes the effective N and can shift p-values by many orders of magnitude. SignalFold exposes this as a setting precisely because it is consequential; the default reproduces the established reference behaviour.
  • Reference dependence. The built-in markers are curated for brain cell types. Applied to another tissue without swapping the marker file, the map is meaningless — not wrong-looking, just uninformative. Supply tissue-matched markers for non-brain work.

11.8 The assumptions that can bite — a checklist

Read before you over-trust a result

None of these invalidate the pipeline; they are the conditions under which its outputs mean what you think.

  • Correlation is not causation. Modules, hubs, and trait links are associations. A hub is a strong lead, never a proven driver.
  • Batch effects are your responsibility. Normalization removes simple per-sample offsets, but if your two groups were run on different days/instruments/batches, that confound can masquerade as biology. (Covariate/batch correction exists in the design but is gated off by default.) Randomise and record batch up front.
  • Missing values are filtered, not modelled. Proteins too sparsely measured are dropped rather than imputed. That is conservative and safe, but a protein absent in one whole group (biologically real on/off behaviour) can be filtered out before it's ever tested.
  • Small samples limit everything. t-tests, correlations, and module stability all degrade with few replicates. With n of 3–4 per group, read results as hypotheses.
  • Multiplicity is controlled within, not across, stages. Each stage's FDR is internally valid; the pipeline does not apply one grand correction spanning DE + GO + cell type. Interpret the whole as an integrated discovery workflow, not a single confirmatory test.
  • Discovery ≠ validation. Everything here generates hypotheses from one dataset. Confirming them needs an independent cohort or a targeted experiment — the scientific non-negotiable.
  • Identity hygiene matters. Gene-symbol cleanup is essential but imperfect; a mis-mapped or split identifier silently distorts counts in every enrichment. Spot-check that your key proteins carry the names you expect.

11.10 Glossary

Abundance / expression
How much of a protein is present. The quantity every stage reasons about.
Proteome
The full set of proteins present in a sample.
Peptide
A fragment of a protein; what mass spectrometers actually measure.
Feature
SignalFold's neutral word for “a row” — a protein or a peptide.
Cohort
One of the two groups being compared (e.g. Control, Disease).
Replicate
A repeated sample within a group; the basis of statistical confidence.
Trait
An optional clinical measurement per sample (age, tau, a severity score).
Normalization
Adjusting samples to be fairly comparable by removing technical offsets.
log₂ fold change
Size and direction of a change on a log₂ scale; +1 = doubled.
p-value
Probability that chance alone would produce a result this extreme if nothing were truly different.
FDR / q-value
p-value adjusted for testing many features; the expected false fraction among your hits.
t-test / Wilcoxon
Tests for whether two groups differ — by means (t) or by ranks (Wilcoxon).
Volcano plot
Scatter of fold change (x) vs significance (y); hits sit up and to the sides.
WGCNA
Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis — groups co-moving proteins into modules.
bicor
Biweight midcorrelation: a robust, outlier-resistant correlation.
Soft power (β)
Exponent applied to correlations to mute weak links and keep strong ones.
Scale-free
A network shape with a few highly-connected hubs and many sparsely-connected nodes.
TOM
Topological Overlap Measure: closeness based on shared neighbours, not just direct correlation.
Module
A colour-named group of proteins that move together.
Eigengene
A module's single representative profile (its first principal component).
kME / hub
How tightly a protein follows its module; high-kME proteins are hubs.
Gene Ontology (GO)
A curated catalogue of protein jobs: Biological Process, Molecular Function, Cellular Component.
Over-representation / Fisher's exact
Testing whether a group contains more members of a category than chance predicts.
Background / universe
The reference set enrichment is measured against (here, measured proteins).
GSEA
An alternative enrichment method that uses the full ranked list, not a cut membership list.
Marker gene
A protein characteristically abundant in one cell type; a cell-type name tag.
NPX
Olink's relative, log₂-scaled protein-abundance unit.
GMT
A simple file format listing gene sets (a term name followed by its genes).
